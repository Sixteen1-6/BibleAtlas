Literary devices
SET SIDE BY SIDE
widely agreed
He had two wives, one named Hannah and the other Peninnah. And Peninnah had children, but Hannah had none.

The narrator sets the two wives side by side: one has children and taunts; the other has none, and prays.

Named by Bullinger, Matthew Henry and Keil and Delitzsch.

No book of figures from 1930 or earlier names this as a figure; it rests on the writers named here.

MIRROR
some interpreters
one named Hannah and the other Peninnah.
And Peninnah had children, but Hannah had none.

The names cross: Hannah, Peninnah, then Peninnah, Hannah. The verse is itself built as a small mirror.

חַנָּה
chan.nah
פְּנִנָּה
pe.nin.nah

The Hebrew keeps the same order: Hannah, Peninnah, Peninnah, Hannah.

Named by Bullinger.

What it is, in Bullinger's words: This is when the parallel lines are so placed that if there be six lines, the first corresponds with the sixth, the second with the fifth, and the third with the fourth Figures of Speech Used in the Bible (1898), p. 356
