OPPOSITE LINES
widely agreed · 3 writers
For the LORD guards the path of the righteous,
but the way of the wicked will perish.

The second line answers the first with its opposite: one way the LORD keeps, the other comes to nothing.

In Hebrew both lines use one word, derekh, “way”. The BSB says “path”, then “way”.

What it is, in Bullinger's words: This is when the words are contrasted in the two or more lines, being opposed in sense the one to the other Figures of Speech Used in the Bible (1898), p. 351

Antithetic parallelism. Bullinger's name for the form: “Antithetic or Opposite”. Behind this note: Both lines turn on one Hebrew word for “way”, and the BSB's “but” marks the turn. Each writer states the contrast on this verse in his own words. Sources: Matthew Henry, Keil and Delitzsch and John Gill on Psalm 1:6. Suggest a correction

SET SIDE BY SIDE
widely agreed · 4 writers
He is like a tree planted by streams of water,
Not so the wicked!
For they are like chaff driven off by the wind.
Psalm 1:3, 4

The psalm sets two kinds of people side by side: the one who delights in the LORD's law, like a fruitful tree, and the wicked, like chaff in the wind.

The psalm marks the turn itself: “Not so the wicked!” (1:4).

No book of figures from 1930 or earlier names this as a figure; it rests on the writers named here.

Juxtaposition: the whole psalm sets the righteous against the wicked. Behind this note: The tree of verse 3 and the chaff of verse 4 are the psalm's own pictures of the two; verse 6 holds both in one verse. Sources: Matthew Henry and Adam Clarke on Psalm 1:1; Keil and Delitzsch on Psalm 1:4; Spurgeon on Psalm 1. Suggest a correction
