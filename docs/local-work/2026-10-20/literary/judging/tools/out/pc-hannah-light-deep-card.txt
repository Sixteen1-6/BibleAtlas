Literary devices
SET SIDE BY SIDE
widely agreed · 3 writers
He had two wives, one named Hannah and the other Peninnah. And Peninnah had children, but Hannah had none.

The narrator sets the two wives side by side: one has children and taunts; the other has none, and prays.

No book of figures from 1930 or earlier names this as a figure; it rests on the writers named here.

Character juxtaposition: a foil, each woman showing up the other. Behind this note: The verse itself joins them with “but”. Bullinger lays the sentence out so the two names answer each other; Henry and Keil and Delitzsch state the contrast. A foil is a reading of the story, and Henry is frank about both women: “Peninnah could not bear the blessing of fruitfulness, but she grew haughty and insolent; Hannah could not bear the affliction of barrenness, but she grew melancholy and discontented.” The card does not make Hannah faultless. Sources: Bullinger's Figures of Speech (1898), Parallelism, introverted, p. 357 on 1 Samuel 1:2; Matthew Henry on 1 Samuel 1:1–8; Keil and Delitzsch on 1 Samuel 1:2. Suggest a correction

MIRROR
some interpreters · 1 writer
one named Hannah and the other Peninnah.
And Peninnah had children, but Hannah had none.

The names cross: Hannah, Peninnah, then Peninnah, Hannah. The verse is itself built as a small mirror.

חַנָּה
chan.nah
פְּנִנָּה
pe.nin.nah

The Hebrew keeps the same order: Hannah, Peninnah, Peninnah, Hannah.

What it is, in Bullinger's words: This is when the parallel lines are so placed that if there be six lines, the first corresponds with the sixth, the second with the fifth, and the third with the fourth Figures of Speech Used in the Bible (1898), p. 356

A crossing sentence. Bullinger: introverted parallelism. Behind this note: The two names come back in reverse order, in the BSB and in the Hebrew. Sources: Bullinger's Figures of Speech (1898), Parallelism, introverted, p. 357 on 1 Samuel 1:2. Suggest a correction
