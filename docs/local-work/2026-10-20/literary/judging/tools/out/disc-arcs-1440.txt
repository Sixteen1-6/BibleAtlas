Psalm 1 sets two ways side by side. The man whose delight is in the Law of the LORD is like a tree planted by streams of water; the wicked are like chaff driven off by the wind. The last verse puts both ways into two lines joined by but.

Widely agreed: named by Matthew Henry, Adam Clarke, Keil and Delitzsch and Spurgeon.

Opposite lines: two lines set against each other in meaning.

As defined in Bullinger’s Figures of Speech (1898), p. 351

The righteous, verses 1–3

He is like a tree planted by streams of water,

The wicked, verses 4–5

Not so the wicked! For they are like chaff driven off by the wind.

Verse 6: both ways

For the LORD guards the path of the righteous,
but the way of the wicked will perish.

Psalm 1 at Simple
The map zooms to Psalm 1, one verse at a time. One long arc joins the psalm’s two halves; a short arc joins the tree (1:3) and the chaff (1:4); a small arc inside verse 6 joins its two lines.
Four writers state the two ways (widely agreed), so it shows at Simple, in plain words with no term. Their names are on the card at every level, each linked to his own page on Psalm 1.
What “opposite lines” means is credited too: Bullinger’s definition, with the page of his 1898 book it is on.
The verses stand in the BSB’s own poetry lines. The lines make no claim and need no writer.
The arc grammar
Same idea, said twice: arcs of one span, side by side (A B C, A′ B′ C′).
Mirror: nested arcs, outer pair first, the turn in the middle (A B C, C′ B′ A′).
Taking turns: crossing arcs (A B A′ B′). Whole-psalm outlines show only at Deep, faintly.
Building up: a stair of rising marks inside the verse.
Set side by side: an ember arc between two people’s verses.
A lens rises from the map when a form sits inside one or two verses.
